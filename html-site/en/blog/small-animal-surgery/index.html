Small Animal Surgery: From Pre-Op Checks to Going Home

24 September 2026 · Dr. Gökay Yeşilovalı, Veterinary Surgeon

When your vet says “this needs surgery”, the first question on your mind is probably “Is it risky?” Small animal surgery covers a wide range of operations in cats and dogs, from spaying and neutering to removing swallowed objects, and how safe an operation is depends largely on what happens before and after the first incision. This article walks you through how we plan an operation at our clinic in Gönyeli, what happens on the day, and what to watch for at home. If you live in Nicosia or Gönyeli, you can complete every stage of the process at a single address.

In short

  • Small animal surgery covers operations in cats and dogs, from spaying and neutering to removing swallowed objects.
  • Most of what makes surgery safe happens beforehand: examination, blood tests and imaging where needed.
  • Food is withheld for at least 8 hours before the operation; follow the vet’s instructions on water and medication.
  • After routine operations most patients go home the same day.
  • Recovery from a routine spay or neuter usually takes 7–10 days; the cone and the pain relief plan matter.

What Does Small Animal Surgery Cover?

Operations in cats and dogs generally fall into two groups. Which group an operation belongs to decides how soon it happens and how the preparation runs.

Planned operations

These are procedures whose date we choose together with you. The most common is spaying or neutering; for timing and benefits, see our guide to spaying and neutering cats and dogs. Removing lumps that grow under the skin and taking biopsies also belong here. Because there is time to prepare, blood tests and imaging can be completed without rushing.

Operations that cannot wait

Some situations cannot be left for days: a swallowed object blocking the intestine, a womb infection in an unspayed female dog, wounds and abscesses that will not heal, injuries after a road accident, or a blocked urinary tract. In male cats in particular, being unable to pass urine can become serious within hours; the signs are in our article on urinary blockage in cats.

In every case we look at non-surgical options first. We recommend an operation when it will genuinely change the outcome.

Before the Operation: The Preparation That Decides Safety

A good operation starts with knowing the patient. The same procedure is planned differently for a healthy two-year-old dog and for a twelve-year-old cat with raised kidney values.

Examination and blood tests

Your pet is examined from nose to tail before the operation. We recommend blood tests for every patient who will have an anaesthetic, because the kidneys and liver are the organs that clear anaesthetic drugs from the body, and their values change the protocol directly. We explain which values we look at in our article on blood tests for cats and dogs; the tests are run in our in-house laboratory.

Imaging

When a swallowed object, a suspected fracture or an abdominal mass is involved, the surgical area needs to be seen in advance. X-ray and ultrasound show the object's position, its size and its effect on the surrounding tissue, so there are no surprises on the operating table.

Fasting and medication

Food is withheld for at least 8 hours before the operation. An empty stomach lowers the risk of vomiting and of material entering the airway under anaesthesia. Your vet will advise you separately about water and any regular medication. Which medicine to give, what time to arrive and how long you will be at the clinic are all explained when the appointment is confirmed.

What Happens on the Day?

Anaesthesia and monitoring

The anaesthetic is planned around your pet's weight, age and test results. Throughout the operation, depth of anaesthesia, breathing, heart rate and body temperature are monitored continuously. Every operation is carried out personally by Dr. Gökay Yeşilovalı, who specialises in small animal surgery.

Recovery and going home

Once the procedure is over, your pet wakes up in a warmed area under observation. After routine operations most patients go home the same day; after more extensive procedures the observation period may be longer. Before you leave, the pain relief plan, wound care and use of the protective cone are explained to you.

Caring for Your Pet at Home

The second half of an operation happens at home, and during this time your observations are your vet's most important source of information.

Pain control and rest

Cats and dogs tend to bear pain quietly. Hiding, loss of appetite, not wanting to be touched or constant licking can all be signs of pain. Give pain relief at the times and for the length of time prescribed, and do not stop it on your own even if your pet seems well. Avoid running, jumping and stairs for the first few days.

Wound care and the protective cone

If the wound is licked, stitches can open and the wound can become infected. Keep the cone on until your vet tells you to remove it. Check the wound once a day and do not wash it. Recovery from a routine spay or neuter usually takes 7–10 days; a follow-up appointment for the stitch check is booked from the start.

When should you call us?

Call us without waiting if you see redness, discharge, swelling or opening at the wound, loss of appetite lasting more than 24 hours, repeated vomiting or unexpected lethargy. A small problem caught early stays small. For serious signs outside opening hours, see our emergency service page.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is small animal surgery risky?

Every anaesthetic carries some risk, but that risk is largely managed through preparation. A pre-operative examination, blood tests and continuous monitoring during the operation make routine procedures safe in healthy animals.

Can my older cat or dog have surgery?

Age alone is not a barrier; overall health is what matters. In older patients the kidneys, liver and heart are assessed with blood tests and, where needed, imaging, and the anaesthetic protocol is adjusted accordingly.

When should my pet stop eating before surgery?

Food is withheld for at least 8 hours before the operation. Follow your vet’s instructions on water and regular medication; these are explained in detail when the appointment is made.

Can we go home on the same day?

After routine operations most patients are discharged the same day, once their recovery from anaesthesia has been observed. After more extensive procedures the observation period at the clinic may be longer.

How long does recovery take?

Recovery from a routine spay or neuter usually takes 7–10 days. It can take longer after more extensive operations; a stitch check and follow-up appointment are planned for every patient.
