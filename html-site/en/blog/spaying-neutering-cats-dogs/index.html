Spaying and Neutering Cats and Dogs: Everything You Need to Know

29 July 2026 · Dr. Gökay Yeşilovalı, Veterinarian

What Is Spaying and Neutering?

Spaying and neutering surgically end your pet's ability to reproduce. In females it means removing the ovaries and uterus (ovariohysterectomy); in males it means removing the testicles (orchiectomy). Performed under general anaesthesia, it is one of the most common, routine, and safe operations carried out in clinics.

The procedure does more than prevent unwanted litters — it protects your pet's health and improves their quality of life. So why does it matter so much?

Why Does It Matter So Much?

For health:

  • In females, it greatly reduces the risk of uterine infection (pyometra) and mammary tumours — protection is highest when done before the first heat cycle.
  • In males, it lowers the risk of testicular cancer and the prostate problems common in later life.
  • It reduces fighting and mating behaviours through which contagious diseases spread.

For behaviour:

  • In males, urine marking, the urge to roam, and aggression are noticeably reduced.
  • In females, the restlessness, excessive vocalising, and urge to escape linked to heat cycles disappear.

For the community: Spaying and neutering are the most effective and humane way to prevent the rise in the number of stray animals.

When Should It Be Done?

The best timing depends on species, breed, and size. For cats, 5–6 months of age is usually ideal. For dogs, small breeds can often be done around 6 months, while large breeds may benefit from waiting until skeletal and joint development is complete. Rather than a fixed rule, your vet's assessment tailored to your pet is the best guide.

How Does the Operation Work?

  • Before: A general examination and, where needed, blood tests are done to improve anaesthetic safety. Your pet comes in on an empty stomach.
  • During: The procedure is carried out under sterile conditions and general anaesthesia. Duration varies by species and sex.
  • After: Most pets are discharged the same day and start to recover quickly.

Post-Operative Care

  • Use a protective collar or surgical suit to stop your pet licking the wound.
  • Avoid excessive activity, running, and jumping in the first few days.
  • Give the pain relief and, if prescribed, antibiotics regularly as directed by your vet.
  • Check the incision daily; call our clinic if you notice redness, swelling, or discharge. Heavy bleeding, an opened incision, or persistent vomiting needs immediate veterinary attention.
  • Sutures are usually checked within 10–14 days.

Common Myths

"It makes them fat." Metabolism slows a little, but weight gain is not inevitable — portion control and exercise keep your pet at an ideal weight.

"It changes their personality." Playfulness, loyalty, and character remain. Only hormone-driven behaviours (marking, roaming, fighting) are reduced.

"Let them have one litter first." There is no health benefit to this. On the contrary, earlier spaying provides stronger protection against risks such as mammary tumours.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best age for spaying or neutering?

For cats, 5–6 months of age is generally suitable. For dogs, breed and size matter: small breeds can often be done around 6 months, while large breeds may benefit from waiting until skeletal development is complete. Your vet will advise the best timing for your pet.

Is the operation risky?

Spaying and neutering are among the most common routine operations performed in clinics and are very safe with modern anaesthesia. A pre-operative examination and, where needed, blood tests minimise anaesthetic risk. As with any surgery there are small risks, but the health benefits far outweigh them.

How long does recovery take?

Most pets start to recover the same or the next day. Sutures are usually checked within 10–14 days. Using a protective collar or surgical suit to prevent licking, avoiding excessive activity, and following your vet's advice all speed up recovery.

Will spaying or neutering make my pet fat?

Metabolism slows down slightly after the operation, but weight gain is not inevitable. Portion control, the right diet, and regular exercise keep your pet at an ideal weight. Personality and character do not change — only hormone-driven behaviours are reduced.
