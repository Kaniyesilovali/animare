Bladder Stones: When Is Cystotomy the Answer?

19 October 2026 · Dr. Gökay Yeşilovalı, Veterinarian

If your cat keeps visiting the litter tray and leaves only a few drops, or you notice blood in your dog's urine, bladder stones are one possible cause. Some stones dissolve and shrink with a special diet; others never will. For that second group, surgery is the most reliable route: cystotomy opens the bladder and removes the stones completely, in one go. This article explains how bladder stones form, when they need surgery and what to expect at home after a cystotomy.

In short

  • The two most common stone types in cats and dogs are struvite and calcium oxalate.
  • Struvite stones can often be dissolved with diet; calcium oxalate stones cannot.
  • Cystotomy is a soft tissue operation that opens the bladder and removes the stones in one go.
  • A stone that blocks the urethra is an emergency, especially in male cats and dogs.
  • Preventing recurrence takes a diet matched to the stone type, more water and regular urine checks.

What Are Bladder Stones and Why Do They Form?

Bladder stones (uroliths) form when minerals in the urine settle out as crystals and then build up into stones. Two types are most common in cats and dogs:

  • Struvite stones: In dogs they usually form alongside a urinary tract infection; in cats they can develop without one. They can dissolve with a suitable urinary diet, plus infection treatment if needed.
  • Calcium oxalate stones: They do not dissolve with diet. If they cause signs or keep growing, they need to be removed.

Drinking too little, urine sitting in the bladder for long periods, excess weight, breed predisposition and feeding habits all raise the risk.

Which Signs Point to Bladder Stones?

The signs often look like a urinary tract infection, so the two are told apart by examination and tests, not by guesswork:

  • Frequent urination in small amounts
  • Straining or pain when urinating
  • Bloody or cloudy urine
  • Urinating outside the litter tray or usual spot
  • Frequent licking of the genital area

Warning: If your pet strains but passes no urine at all, a small stone may have moved into the urethra and blocked it. This mostly happens in male cats and dogs and can become life-threatening within hours. Read more in our article on urinary blockage in cats, and contact our emergency service without delay.

How Are Bladder Stones Diagnosed?

Urine and blood tests

A urine test shows crystals, infection and how acidic the urine is. A blood test checks kidney values and forms the basis of the anaesthetic plan if surgery is needed. These tests are processed the same day in our in-house laboratory. Our blood test guide explains what each value shows.

X-ray and ultrasound

Calcium oxalate and struvite stones usually show up clearly on X-ray. Ultrasound picks up small stones that X-rays miss, as well as thickening of the bladder wall. The number, size and position of the stones are confirmed with diagnostic imaging, and the treatment decision is based on that information.

When Is Cystotomy Needed?

Not every bladder stone needs surgery. If the stone looks like struvite and your pet is comfortable, dissolving it with diet is tried first. Cystotomy comes to the fore when:

  • The stone type points to one that diet cannot dissolve (e.g. calcium oxalate)
  • The stones have not shrunk after a few weeks of dietary treatment
  • The stones are large or numerous
  • They cause repeated urinary tract infections
  • A stone is blocking the urethra or is at risk of doing so

In short, cystotomy is the choice when the stones need to go without waiting, and for good.

How Is a Cystotomy Performed?

Cystotomy is a soft tissue operation done under general anaesthesia. The bladder is reached through an incision in the abdomen, opened, and the stones are removed. The bladder and urethra are then flushed to reduce the chance of small fragments being left behind. The bladder and abdominal wall are closed in layers.

The removed stones are sent for analysis to confirm their type. This result matters, because the diet and follow-up plan that prevent recurrence are chosen according to the stone type. Breathing, heart rate and body temperature are monitored continuously throughout the operation. Our small animal surgery guide walks through the whole day of an operation step by step.

Recovery After Surgery

In the first days after a cystotomy, it is normal to see:

  • Slight blood in the urine and frequent urination (usually settling within a few days)
  • Mild tenderness around the wound

At home:

  • Use the protective collar so the wound is not licked.
  • Give pain relief and any other medication exactly as planned.
  • Restrict activity for the first days; avoid jumping and running.
  • Do not miss the follow-up appointment for the stitch check.

Call us straight away if you see discharge or swelling at the wound, if no urine is passing, or if your pet becomes lethargic and stops eating.

Preventing Bladder Stones from Coming Back

Surgery removes the stones but not the conditions that formed them. There are three key steps to prevent recurrence:

  1. A diet matched to the stone type: A urinary diet chosen on the basis of the stone analysis slows down new crystal formation. Our nutrition consulting service can help you choose the right food.
  2. More water: Wet food, more than one water bowl or a water fountain helps dilute the urine.
  3. Regular checks: Urine tests and imaging in the months after surgery catch new stones early.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can bladder stones be treated without surgery?

Some can. Struvite stones can dissolve with a suitable urinary diet, together with infection treatment if needed. Calcium oxalate stones do not dissolve with diet; if they cause signs, they need to be removed by cystotomy.

Is cystotomy a risky operation?

Cystotomy is a routine soft tissue operation, commonly performed in cats and dogs. A pre-operative blood test and continuous monitoring during surgery reduce the risk considerably. The real danger is waiting until a stone blocks the urethra.

How long is recovery after cystotomy?

Blood in the urine and frequent urination usually settle within a few days. Allow about two weeks for the wound to heal and the stitches to come out. During this time your pet wears a protective collar and activity is restricted.

Can bladder stones come back after surgery?

They can. That is why the removed stone is sent for analysis and a diet plan is built around its type. Plenty of water and regular urine checks lower the risk of recurrence considerably.

How can I tell if my cat has bladder stones?

The most common signs are frequent trips to the litter tray with little urine, straining, blood in the urine and urinating outside the tray. A definite diagnosis needs a urine test and imaging. If your cat cannot pass any urine at all, it is an emergency and you should see a vet straight away.
