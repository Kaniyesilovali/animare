Chronic Kidney Disease in Cats: Early Signs and Care

5 August 2026 · Dr. Gökay Yeşilovalı, Veterinarian

Your cat is drinking more than before, the litter tray fills up faster, and they feel lighter when you pick them up — this may not be ordinary ageing. Chronic kidney disease is one of the most common conditions we see in older cats, and its defining feature is how quietly it progresses.

What Is Chronic Kidney Disease?

The kidneys filter the blood, remove waste through urine, balance water and minerals, and help regulate blood pressure and red blood cell production. In chronic kidney disease, the units that do this filtering are permanently lost over time.

Here is the crucial part: the kidney is remarkably good at compensating with the tissue it has left. Until a large share of that tissue is gone, a cat can look perfectly well and routine blood values can still come back normal. By the time signs appear, the disease has usually been present for a while. That is why early diagnosis depends far more on regular check-ups than on what an owner can spot at home.

Early Signs: What to Watch For

As the kidneys lose the ability to concentrate urine, a cat passes more urine and drinks more to replace the lost fluid. The first changes are usually these:

  • Drinking more — the water bowl emptying faster, or a new interest in taps and shower trays.
  • Urinating more — larger and more frequent clumps, a litter tray that needs changing sooner.
  • Slow weight loss — most visible along the spine and hips.
  • A smaller, fussier appetite — sniffing at a food they used to love and walking away.
  • A dull, unkempt coat — the cat grooming less than they used to.
  • Low energy, sleeping more, no longer jumping to high places.
  • Occasional vomiting, bad breath, mouth ulcers. These belong to the later stages.

None of these signs is unique to kidney disease — diabetes and an overactive thyroid produce a similar picture. Blood and urine tests are the only way to tell them apart.

Which Cats Are at Risk?

  • Age: The single biggest risk factor. Risk starts to climb after the age of 7 and becomes marked past 10.
  • Breed: Persians, Exotic Shorthairs, and their crosses can carry inherited polycystic kidney disease. A predisposition has also been reported in breeds such as the Maine Coon, Siamese, and Abyssinian.
  • Previous urinary problems: Especially male cats who have had a urinary blockage.
  • High blood pressure: It damages the kidney, and kidney disease raises blood pressure — each feeds the other.
  • Toxins: Lilies (deadly to cats), antifreeze, and painkillers not intended for cats cause lasting kidney damage.
  • Dental disease, repeated infections, and long illnesses that lead to dehydration.

How Is It Diagnosed?

No single test can assess kidney health on its own. In the clinic we usually combine these steps:

  • Blood tests: Creatinine and urea (BUN) are the classic kidney values. SDMA is a marker that can reflect a drop in kidney function earlier.
  • Urine tests: Every bit as important as the blood work. Urine concentration shows how well the kidney holds on to water, and protein loss in the urine tells us about the likely course of the disease.
  • Blood pressure measurement: High blood pressure can quietly damage the eyes and brain, so it is measured at diagnosis and at every follow-up.
  • Ultrasound and x-rays: To assess kidney size and structure and to look for stones or cysts.

The results are read together to stage the disease. The stage shapes both the treatment and how often your cat needs checking: diet changes lead in the early stages, while medication and fluid support take on more weight later.

Treatment: Slow It Down, Keep Them Comfortable

Lost kidney tissue does not come back. What can be done — and done well — is to slow the disease and keep your cat feeling like themselves. The main elements are:

  • A renal diet: Foods with reduced phosphorus and balanced, high-quality protein are the strongest single step you can take. Spread the change over 7–10 days; cats do not accept sudden food swaps.
  • More water: Wet food, wide bowls in several rooms, a water fountain. For a cat with kidney disease, dehydration is one of the biggest risks.
  • Phosphate binders: Added to food when diet alone is not enough.
  • Blood pressure and protein-loss treatment: Prescribed when needed to take load off the kidney.
  • Anti-nausea and appetite support: Keeping your cat eating is the most critical part of the plan.
  • Subcutaneous fluids: In advanced stages, these can be given at home once your vet has shown you how.

Never give any medication without veterinary advice. Painkillers made for people are among the most frequent causes of kidney failure in cats.

Home Monitoring: What to Write Down

  • Weight once a week, on the same scales — weight loss is the first warning signal.
  • How much food is eaten each day, and any change in appetite.
  • The number and size of clumps in the litter tray, and how often the water bowl empties.
  • The date any new sign appears: vomiting, diarrhoea, bad breath, lethargy.

These simple notes let us fine-tune treatment at the check-up. A short note on your phone is enough.

When Not to Wait

In a cat with kidney disease, the following need urgent veterinary assessment:

  • Eating nothing at all for more than 24 hours.
  • Repeated vomiting with clear lethargy.
  • Mouth ulcers or a sharp odour from the mouth.
  • Signs of dehydration: sunken eyes, skin at the scruff that is slow to fall back into place.
  • Passing no urine, or a sudden drop in the amount.
  • Sudden blindness, unsteady walking, or permanently dilated pupils — these can point to high blood pressure.

Catching It Early: Senior Check-Ups

In chronic kidney disease, the stage at which it is caught matters more than anything else. A cat diagnosed early can live comfortably for years with the right diet and follow-up. That is why we recommend blood and urine tests once a year from the age of 7, and every six months from the age of 10. Keeping lilies out of the house and making sure fresh water is always within reach are simple, effective protection too.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the first sign of kidney disease in cats?

In most cats the first change owners notice is drinking and urinating more. If the litter tray fills up faster and the water bowl empties more often, take it as a warning. Slow weight loss, a smaller appetite, and a dull coat usually follow. Because these changes creep in over weeks, they are easy to miss at home.

Can chronic kidney disease be cured?

Lost kidney tissue cannot be regained, so the disease cannot be cured. It can, however, be slowed down with the right diet, better water intake, and control of blood pressure and phosphorus. Many cats diagnosed at an early stage go on to live comfortably for years.

What should a cat with kidney disease eat?

A veterinary renal diet is one of the single most effective steps you can take. These foods have reduced phosphorus and a balanced amount of high-quality protein. Wet food is preferred because it increases water intake. Change food gradually over 7–10 days — cats rarely accept an abrupt switch.

How often should my cat's kidney values be checked?

For healthy cats we recommend blood and urine tests once a year from the age of 7, and every six months from the age of 10 or in cats with risk factors. For cats already diagnosed, the stage of disease and the response to treatment set the check-up interval.
