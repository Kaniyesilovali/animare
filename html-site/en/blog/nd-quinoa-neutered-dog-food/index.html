Food for Neutered Dogs: N&D Quinoa Neutered

4 September 2026 · Dr. Gökay Yeşilovalı, Veterinary Surgeon

The sentence we hear most often after a neutering operation is this: “I am feeding the same food, the same amount, and he still put on weight.” It is not an illusion. After surgery a dog's daily energy requirement genuinely falls, while appetite usually does not. Within a few months the gap shows up on the scales. N&D Quinoa Neutered is an everyday food for neutered dogs, built to close exactly that gap. This article covers what is in the bag, where it differs from a standard adult formula, who it suits and who it does not, and why the portion matters more than the brand.

In short

  • Daily energy needs fall by roughly 20-30% after neutering, while appetite usually stays the same.
  • N&D Quinoa Neutered is a grain-free everyday food built on duck, with quinoa, broccoli and asparagus.
  • Compared with a standard adult formula, fat is clearly lower and fibre roughly double — fewer calories, longer fullness.
  • The formula includes L-carnitine, glucosamine and chondroitin sulphate.
  • Duck-based, but it contains chicken fat: it is not a substitute for an elimination diet where chicken allergy is suspected.
  • Portions are set by target weight, not current weight, and treats should stay under 10% of daily calories.
  • For diagnosed obesity this food is not enough on its own; a vet-supervised weight-loss programme is needed.

What Changes After Neutering?

Sex hormones influence more than reproduction: they also shape energy expenditure and appetite control. After the operation three things happen at once — baseline energy expenditure drops, the sense of fullness weakens, and most dogs become slightly less active. The measured result is a fall of around a quarter in daily energy requirement. Because the bowl stays the same, that shortfall is written straight into fat tissue.

Weight gain is fastest in the months immediately after surgery. Putting off the food adjustment until “he starts to look heavy” turns an easy job into a hard one: taking weight off always takes longer than keeping it off. For detail on the operation itself, its timing and recovery, see our article on spaying and neutering cats and dogs.

Excess weight is not a cosmetic matter. It loads the joints, raises anaesthetic risk in surgery, makes breathing harder in hot weather and sets the stage for insulin resistance. Through the long Cyprus summer, an overweight dog copes with heat markedly worse than one at its ideal weight.

How “Neutered” Formulas Differ

The logic behind foods made for neutered animals is simple: cut the calories, keep the fullness. Three adjustments do the work.

  • Fat is reduced. Fat carries more calories per gram than any other nutrient, so it is the most direct lever on calorie density.
  • Fibre is increased. Fibre fills the stomach without carrying calories. The same volume of food holds fewer calories and the dog still feels full — this is what reduces the begging that follows an empty bowl.
  • Protein is preserved per calorie. If protein is cut along with calories, part of the weight lost is muscle. The aim is to limit fat while protecting lean mass.

Many such formulas also contain L-carnitine, which helps shuttle fatty acids for burning inside the cell, plus glucosamine and chondroitin for joint support. What Is in N&D Quinoa Neutered?

Duck is the backbone of the formula: boneless duck and dehydrated duck protein together make up around 40% of the recipe. Sweet potato replaces grain as the carbohydrate source, and quinoa — the ingredient that names the line — sits at roughly 8%.

  • Quinoa (8%). Botanically a seed rather than a grain, classed as a pseudo-cereal. It contributes plant protein with a balanced amino acid profile plus fibre, at a lower glycaemic load than cereals.
  • Broccoli and asparagus (2.5% each). Added for fibre and antioxidant content. Asparagus is also among the plants traditionally counted as supporting fluid balance.
  • Pea fibre, psyllium and fructo-oligosaccharides. A fibre blend for satiety and gut transit; FOS belongs to the prebiotic group that feeds large-intestine flora.
  • Herring protein and fish oil. The omega-3 source. The contribution of EPA and DHA to skin, coat and joint health is well described.
  • Linseed, dried egg and beet pulp. Omega-3, highly digestible protein, and fibre that firms up stool consistency, respectively.
  • L-carnitine, glucosamine and chondroitin sulphate. Added for fat metabolism and joint support.

The formula also contains chicken fat, and labels in some countries list dehydrated chicken too. That detail matters, because most owners who read “duck recipe” assume it is a chicken-free option. For a dog under investigation for chicken allergy, this food does not replace an elimination diet.

Mini, or Medium & Maxi?

On the canine side, Quinoa Neutered comes in two kibble sizes: Adult Mini for small breeds and Adult Medium & Maxi for medium and large ones. The recipe logic is the same; what differs is kibble size and small adjustments to mineral balance for body size.

Choose by your dog's adult weight. Large kibble given to a small breed encourages swallowing instead of chewing, and small kibble given to a large breed is swallowed almost whole — both reduce digestibility and the modest mechanical benefit the tooth surface gets from chewing.

Who It Is Not For

To be straight about it, not every neutered dog needs to change food. In the following situations something else is the better choice:

  • Puppies and dogs still growing. Calories are not restricted during growth; a puppy formula is used instead.
  • Thin, inappetent or convalescing dogs. Here the goal is taking calories in, not cutting them.
  • Working and highly active dogs. Even when neutered, a dog with high energy expenditure will not be well served by a reduced-calorie formula.
  • Diagnosed obesity. This is an everyday food, not a therapeutic diet. Clear excess weight calls for an examination first, then a controlled weight-loss plan, moving to a veterinary diet where needed.
  • Kidney, liver or pancreatic disease, or a history of urinary stones. In these cases food is chosen for the condition. We listed every food group we stock in our article on the cat and dog food range.
  • Dogs under investigation for chicken allergy. Chicken fat in the formula rules it out as an elimination diet.

Portions: Half the Job Is the Scales

No food prevents weight gain when it is served by eye. In a neutered dog, getting the portion right matters more than choosing the brand. Four practical rules:

  • Feed for target weight. Read the table on the bag against the weight you want your dog to reach, not the one on the scales today.
  • Use kitchen scales. Measuring by cup varies by up to 20% depending on how the cup is filled. Scales remove that uncertainty.
  • Count the treats. Treats should stay under 10% of daily calories. A slice of cheese or a handful of biscuits quietly restores everything trimmed from the bowl.
  • Split the daily amount in two. Morning and evening meals spread hunger more evenly than a single serving.

In a multi-pet household, separate the bowls. A restricted portion does not add up if it is being topped up from somebody else's dinner.

How to Change Food Safely

An abrupt change of food is one of the commonest causes of diarrhoea and vomiting. Spread the transition over 7 to 10 days:

  • Days 1-3: one quarter new food, three quarters old.
  • Days 4-6: half and half.
  • Days 7-9: three quarters new food.
  • Day 10: new food only.

Watch stool consistency and appetite throughout. Because fibre is higher, a modest increase in stool volume is expected and normal. But if diarrhoea lasts more than 48 hours, contains blood, or comes with loss of appetite and lethargy, stop the transition and contact the clinic.

Do not change food in the first days after surgery. Appetite is already unsettled after anaesthesia, and introducing two variables at once makes it impossible to tell which caused a problem. Start the transition once your dog is eating normally again.

Keeping Track of Weight

The scales alone are not enough; body condition needs a look too. There are two simple checks you can do at home.

The rib test: rest your palm flat on your dog's ribcage. If you can feel the ribs without pressing — much as you feel the bones on the back of your own hand — the weight is about right. If you have to press to find them, there is excess weight; if they are visible from across the room, the dog is too thin.

The view from above: looking down, there should be a clear waist behind the ribs. From the side, the belly line should tuck upwards from chest to groin. Losing those two lines shows up before the scales do.

Weighing once a month is enough. During weight loss the target is 1-2% of body weight per week; faster than that means muscle is going too. At our clinic, weighing and body condition scoring are a routine part of the general check-up.

In Closing

Weight gain after neutering is not inevitable — it is the result of an equation whose terms have changed. N&D Quinoa Neutered makes one side of that equation easier: fewer calories, more fibre, protein preserved, joints supported. Frequently Asked Questions

Does my neutered dog have to eat a “neutered” food?

It is not compulsory, but it makes the job easier. Daily energy requirements fall by roughly 20-30% after neutering, while appetite usually stays the same or increases. Feeding the same food in a clearly smaller portion also works, but the bowl then looks visibly emptier and the dog may still feel hungry. A formula with reduced calories and added fibre delivers the same fullness for fewer calories. Dogs that stay lean, are very active or work do not need to switch.

Is N&D Quinoa Neutered chicken-free?

The main protein is duck, but the formula also contains chicken fat, and labels in some countries list dehydrated chicken as well. That means it cannot be used as an elimination diet for a dog suspected of a genuine chicken allergy. Where food allergy is suspected, the right approach is a vet-supervised elimination trial; a single named protein on the front of the bag is not enough on its own.

How soon after neutering should I switch?

Usually within the first two weeks, once your dog is eating normally again. The metabolic change begins straight after surgery and weight gain is fastest in the first few months, so switching early is easier than taking weight off later. If your dog is off food in the first days after the operation or is on pain relief, delay the change by a few days rather than introducing two variables at once.

My dog is already overweight. Will this food take the weight off?

This is an everyday food designed to prevent weight gain, not a veterinary diet formulated for diagnosed obesity. If your dog is clearly overweight, an examination comes first: thyroid function, medication and joint pain all change the picture. In a weight-loss programme the target is a loss of 1-2% of body weight per week, with portions calculated on ideal rather than current weight. If needed we move to a Vet Life obesity diet.

How much should I feed per day?

The table on the bag is a starting point, not a prescription. The amount is chosen for the weight you want your dog to reach, not the weight it is at today, and it is adjusted by weighing every four weeks. Split the daily amount into two meals and use kitchen scales rather than a cup. Treats should stay under 10% of daily calories; a handful of biscuits quietly puts back everything you took out of the bowl.

Is quinoa suitable for dogs, and is this food grain-free?

The N&D Quinoa line is grain-free. Quinoa is not botanically a grain but a seed classed as a pseudo-cereal; alongside its starch it provides plant protein with a balanced amino acid profile, plus fibre. It makes up around 8% of the formula, so it is not the main calorie source. The debate linking grain-free diets to heart muscle disease centres on legume-heavy formulas in particular; if your dog has a history of heart disease, discuss food choice with your vet.
