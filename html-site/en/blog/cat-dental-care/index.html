Dental Hygiene for Cats: Tartar, Gums and Home Care

28 May 2026 · Updated 23 August 2026 · Animare Veterinary

A healthy cat's mouth does not smell. If you notice your cat's breath from an arm's length away, that is not "cat breath" — it is usually bacteria living under the gum line. Dental disease is the most common problem we treat in adult cats, and it is also the one owners are least likely to spot, because a cat with a painful mouth will often carry on eating as if nothing were wrong.

In short

  • Most cats over the age of three have some degree of dental disease.
  • Plaque hardens into tartar within days — and once it is hard, brushing no longer removes it.
  • Brushing is the single most effective thing you can do at home. Daily is ideal; three times a week is the realistic minimum.
  • A proper cleaning needs a short general anaesthetic, because the disease sits under the gum line.
  • Bad breath, red gums, drooling, or chewing on one side mean it is time for a check-up.

Why Dental Hygiene Matters More in Cats Than People Think

Dental disease is not only a mouth problem. The inflamed pocket around a tooth root is an open door: bacteria and inflammatory products pass from it into the bloodstream day after day. Over years, that constant low-level load puts extra work on the heart, kidneys, and liver. In older cats it often runs alongside chronic kidney disease, and each condition makes managing the other harder.

The more immediate issue is pain. A tooth with an exposed root or a resorptive lesion hurts in the way a cracked human tooth hurts — constantly. Cats do not stop eating when their mouth is sore; they adapt. They swallow kibble whole, chew on one side, groom less, and become a little quieter. Owners tend to put those changes down to age. After a dental treatment, the most common thing we hear at the follow-up is that the cat has become playful again.

How Dental Disease Develops: Plaque to Periodontitis

Every case follows the same four steps, and the point of home care is to stop it at the first one:

  • Plaque. A soft, invisible film of bacteria settles on the tooth within hours of eating. At this stage a toothbrush wipes it straight off.
  • Tartar (calculus). Minerals in saliva harden that film in a matter of days. Tartar is the yellow-brown crust you can see, it cannot be brushed away, and its rough surface collects new plaque faster.
  • Gingivitis. The gum reacts to the bacteria at the tooth's edge: a red, swollen band along the gum line that bleeds easily. This stage is still fully reversible.
  • Periodontitis. Inflammation spreads to the ligament and bone that hold the tooth. Gums recede, the root is exposed, the tooth loosens. Bone that has been lost does not come back — the goal from here is to stop it going further.

The practical takeaway: the window in which brushing solves the problem is measured in days, not months. That is why frequency matters more than technique.

Signs of Dental Problems in Cats

Book a check-up if you notice any of the following:

  • Bad breath. The earliest and most reliable sign.
  • Yellow-brown tartar, especially on the upper cheek teeth just behind the canines.
  • A red line along the gum line, swollen gums, or blood on a toy or in the water bowl.
  • Chewing on one side, dropping food, or going to the bowl and walking away hungry.
  • Preferring wet food or swallowing kibble whole when they used to crunch it.
  • Drooling — sometimes tinged with blood.
  • Pawing at the mouth, jaw chattering, or turning the head away when you touch that side of the face.
  • Grooming less, a scruffy coat, or a cat that has stopped washing its face.
  • Weight loss with no other explanation.

You can check most of this at home in ten seconds: lift the upper lip while your cat is calm and look at where the gum meets the tooth. Do it once a month and you will notice a change early.

Three Problems That Are Specific to Cats

Cats do not simply get a smaller version of dog dental disease. Three conditions belong to them:

  • Tooth resorption. The tooth's own structure is broken down by the body, usually starting at the gum line. It is common in adult cats and it is painful. A pink defect at the gum line, or a jaw that chatters when that tooth is touched, is the classic sign. The tooth cannot be filled — it has to come out, and dental x-rays show how far the process has reached below the gum.
  • Gingivostomatitis. A severe, painful inflammation of the whole mouth, far out of proportion to the amount of tartar present. These cats drool, refuse dry food, and lose weight. Treatment usually combines full-mouth or partial extractions with medical management; it is one of the few situations where removing teeth makes a cat eat better.
  • Juvenile gingivitis. Bright red gums in a cat under two, often around the time the adult teeth come through. Caught early with cleaning and home care, many of these cats do well; ignored, some progress to early bone loss.

Brushing Your Cat's Teeth: How to Actually Start

Brushing is the only home method that removes plaque before it hardens. Almost every failed attempt has the same cause: the brush appears on day one. Spread the introduction over one to two weeks instead, and give up nothing but a few seconds a day.

  • Days 1–3. Let your cat lick enzymatic cat toothpaste off your finger. Most are poultry or malt flavoured, and this step should feel like a treat, not a procedure.
  • Days 4–7. With paste on your finger, lift the lip and rub along the outer surface of the canines and the big cheek teeth. Two or three seconds a side is plenty.
  • Days 8–14. Swap your finger for a soft cat toothbrush or a finger brush. Hold it at roughly 45° to the gum line and use small circles.
  • From then on. Outer surfaces only — the tongue keeps the inner surfaces reasonably clean, and reaching for them is how you get bitten. Ten to fifteen seconds a side is a complete brush.

Pick a fixed moment: after the evening meal, when your cat is already relaxed. Stop while it is still going well rather than pushing to finish, and never restrain a cat that is genuinely struggling — a bad session costs you a fortnight of progress. Never use human toothpaste. It is made to be spat out, and the fluoride and foaming agents in it upset a cat's stomach when swallowed.

One caution: brushing an already painful mouth will not work and is not fair on the cat. If the gums are red and bleeding, get the mouth treated first, then start home care on healthy tissue.

What Else Works — and What Doesn't

Method How much it helps
Daily brushingThe most effective home measure. Nothing else comes close.
Dental dietUseful. Larger kibble with a fibre structure that wipes the tooth as it sinks in; some also slow tartar formation.
Dental treats and chewsA helpful extra, best given at a fixed time each day. Count them towards the daily calories.
Water additives, gels, spraysA modest support when brushing is impossible. Not a substitute for it.
Ordinary dry foodLittle effect. Standard kibble shatters before the tooth passes through it.
Bones and hard objectsAvoid. A common cause of fractured teeth.
Human toothpasteNever. It is not meant to be swallowed.

What a Professional Dental Cleaning Involves

A veterinary dental is not the same procedure as a human scale and polish, because everything has to happen in one session while the cat is asleep. A typical visit looks like this:

  • Pre-anaesthetic assessment. A physical exam and blood tests to check organ function, particularly kidneys and liver in older cats.
  • Short general anaesthetic with an airway tube and monitoring throughout. The tube also keeps water and bacteria out of the lungs.
  • Tooth-by-tooth charting. Each tooth is probed for pockets, mobility, and resorptive lesions. Much of what matters is invisible from the outside.
  • Dental x-rays where indicated — the only way to see root and bone, and what tells us whether a tooth can be saved.
  • Ultrasonic scaling, above and, crucially, below the gum line, where periodontal disease lives.
  • Polishing. Scaling leaves microscopic scratches on the enamel; polishing smooths them so plaque takes longer to settle.
  • Extractions where needed, under local anaesthetic in addition, with pain relief to go home.

Most cats go home the same day, eat softened food for a few days, and are back to normal within a week. Where extractions have been carried out, they are part of our surgical care and pain relief continues at home.

Why "Anaesthesia-Free Cleaning" Isn't the Same Thing

Scraping visible tartar off an awake cat makes the teeth look whiter and leaves the disease exactly where it was. The plaque under the gum line is untouched, no tooth can be probed, no x-ray can be taken, and the enamel is left scratched and unpolished — so tartar returns faster than before. It is also frightening for the cat, and struggling near sharp instruments carries its own risk. A modern, monitored anaesthetic with pre-operative blood work is the safer option, including for most older cats.

How Often Does a Cat Need Dental Care?

  • At home: brush daily if you can, three times a week at minimum. Lift the lip and check the gum line once a month.
  • Check-up: a mouth assessment at least once a year from the age of three, and at every annual vaccination visit in any case.
  • Professional cleaning: when the examination shows one is needed — for many cats that works out at roughly once a year.
  • More often (every 6–12 months): cats with previous gingivitis, tooth resorption, or gingivostomatitis, and flat-faced breeds such as Persians and Exotic Shorthairs, whose crowded teeth trap plaque.

Kittens: What's Normal and What Isn't

Kittens have 26 milk teeth, which are replaced by 30 adult teeth between roughly three and six months of age. Chewing more than usual, a little blood on a toy, or finding a tiny tooth on the floor during this period is normal — most milk teeth are simply swallowed. What is not normal is a milk canine still sitting beside its adult replacement after six months: that pair traps food and starts tartar early, and the retained tooth is best removed, often at the same time as neutering. Bright red gums in a young cat are never normal either. Handling a kitten's mouth every day for a few seconds is also the cheapest investment you will ever make in their dental care — a cat introduced to brushing at four months rarely objects at four years.

When Not to Wait

Most dental problems can wait for a routine appointment. These need prompt veterinary attention:

  • Refusing all food for more than 24 hours, or wanting to eat but backing away from the bowl.
  • Swelling of the face or under an eye — often a root abscess.
  • Constant drooling, especially with blood.
  • A broken tooth with a pink or dark centre, which means the pulp is exposed.
  • A mouth injury after a fall or a fight, or a jaw that no longer closes properly.
  • An ulcer or mass in the mouth that does not settle within a couple of weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my cat has dental disease?

The earliest reliable sign is bad breath — a healthy cat's mouth should not smell. Lift the lip and look at the gum line: yellow-brown tartar on the teeth, a red band where gum meets tooth, or bleeding when you touch it all point to dental disease. Eating on one side, dropping food, chewing then walking away, drooling, or pawing at the mouth are later signs. Cats hide oral pain well, so many keep eating until the problem is advanced.

Can a cat's teeth be cleaned without anaesthesia?

Not properly. Anaesthesia-free scaling only removes visible tartar from the crown and leaves the plaque under the gum line, which is where periodontal disease actually happens. It also makes it impossible to probe each tooth, take dental x-rays, or polish the enamel, and it is stressful for the cat. A short, monitored general anaesthetic allows the whole mouth to be assessed and cleaned in one session.

How often should a cat have a professional dental cleaning?

Most cats benefit from a dental assessment once a year from about the age of three, with a scale and polish when the check-up shows one is needed. Cats with a history of gingivitis, tooth resorption, or gingivostomatitis, and brachycephalic breeds such as Persians with crowded teeth, may need one every six to twelve months. Cats whose teeth are brushed daily can often go longer between cleanings.

How do I start brushing my cat's teeth?

Go slowly over one to two weeks. Start by letting your cat lick enzymatic cat toothpaste off your finger for a few days, then rub the paste along the outer surface of the canines and cheek teeth, then introduce a soft cat toothbrush or finger brush for a few seconds a side. Brush only the outer surfaces, always after a meal or a calm moment, and stop while your cat is still relaxed. Never use human toothpaste — it is not made to be swallowed.

Does dry food clean a cat's teeth?

Ordinary kibble does very little; most cats crush it without the tooth ever passing through the piece. Dental diets are different: the kibble is larger and has a fibre structure that wipes the tooth as it sinks in, and some also contain a compound that slows tartar formation. A dental diet or a dental treat helps, but neither replaces brushing.

What is tooth resorption in cats?

Tooth resorption is a condition in which the body breaks down the tooth's own structure, usually starting at the gum line. It is one of the most common dental problems in adult cats and it is painful. The typical sign is a pink or red defect at the gum line, or a cat that trembles or chatters its jaw when that tooth is touched. Filling the tooth does not work — the affected tooth needs to be extracted, and dental x-rays show how far the process has gone below the gum.
