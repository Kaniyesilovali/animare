Blood Tests for Cats and Dogs: Why They Matter

29 August 2026 · Dr. Gökay Yeşilovalı, Veterinarian

One question comes up in the consulting room more than almost any other: “He looks completely fine — does he really need a blood test?” It is a fair question. Testing exists to see what an examination cannot: the working capacity of organs you cannot feel with your hands or look at with your eyes, expressed as numbers. This article explains what each test shows, why pre-anaesthetic screening is not something to negotiate over, and how results are actually read.

In short

  • Cats and dogs hide illness; two thirds of kidney tissue can be lost before any sign shows.
  • A blood count shows infection and anaemia, biochemistry shows liver and kidney capacity, urinalysis shows what neither can.
  • Pre-anaesthetic testing sets the drug dose — anaesthetics are broken down by the liver and cleared by the kidneys.
  • A test taken while your pet is well records their personal normal range; its real value appears a year later.
  • On the island, rapid kits for Leishmaniasis and tick-borne disease give answers in minutes.
  • A single borderline value is not a diagnosis; the trend says more than one measurement.

Why Animals Hide Illness

In the wild, looking weak means being hunted. Cats and dogs carry that instinct into the living room: they conceal pain, lethargy and loss of appetite for as long as they can. The first sign an owner notices is usually not the beginning of the illness but the point at which the animal can no longer hide it.

The kidney is the clearest example. Roughly two thirds of the tissue can lose function before visible signs such as increased thirst and urination appear. Blood and urine values, however, start shifting far earlier. The same holds for the liver, thyroid and pancreas. Our article on chronic kidney disease in cats follows that process in detail.

This is why the most useful test is the one taken while your pet is well. Reference ranges are deliberately wide, because breed, age and build vary enormously. If a healthy cat's creatinine sits at the low end of the range, a result at the high end a year later is still “normal” on paper — but it is not normal for that cat. Without an earlier value to compare against, that shift stays invisible.

What Each Test Shows

Complete Blood Count

Counts the cells in the blood: red cells, white cells and platelets. A low red cell count means anaemia, which we see frequently in tick-borne disease and in advanced kidney failure here on the island. A raised white cell count says the body is fighting inflammation somewhere; a markedly low one suggests something more serious affecting the bone marrow. Platelets relate to clotting, which matters especially before surgery.

Biochemistry Panel

Measures how organs are functioning. Urea and creatinine reflect the kidneys, ALT and ALKP the liver, glucose the sugar balance, and total protein and albumin the nutritional and fluid state. In cats we often add thyroid hormone (T4); in older cats hyperthyroidism is a quiet cause of both weight loss and strain on the heart.

Urinalysis

Tells you what blood alone cannot. Urine concentration shows how well the kidney can still concentrate urine, and that ability weakens before blood values change. Protein, crystals, blood and bacteria in the urine separate infection from stone formation and much else besides. In male cats it carries particular weight because of the risk of urinary blockage.

Rapid Test Kits

These look for one specific agent and give an answer within minutes. The ones we use most on the island are for Leishmaniasis, Ehrlichia and Babesia, along with FeLV and FIV in cats and parvovirus in dogs. They do not replace the panels, they complete them: a blood count tells you there is anaemia, and the rapid test tells you whether a tick-borne infection is behind it.

Pre-Anaesthetic Screening: Why It Is Not Optional

Anaesthetic drugs are broken down by the liver and cleared by the kidneys. Choosing a dose without knowing the capacity of those two organs on that particular day risks prolonged recovery, low blood pressure or lasting organ damage — even in an animal that looks entirely healthy from the outside. Screening exists to match the drug to the patient's real capacity.

We are looking for three things: whether the organs can handle the anaesthetic, whether clotting is adequate, and whether there is a hidden infection. If the results are normal, the operation proceeds as planned. If they are not, three options open up — the dose is changed, fluid support is given beforehand, or surgery is postponed until the underlying problem is treated.

This is not reserved for major procedures. The same screening applies to routine planned operations such as spaying and neutering, because what makes a procedure low-risk is not how small it is but how complete the preparation was. It is a standard part of our surgical service.

In Cyprus, Testing Has a Geographical Meaning Too

The Mediterranean climate brings certain diseases to the fore, and most of them can only be distinguished in the laboratory. Leishmaniasis, carried by the sandfly, is a genuine risk here; because its early signs are vague, many cases are caught by a test run during a routine check. Tick-borne Ehrlichiosis and Babesiosis often announce themselves as a low platelet count, and that single finding is enough to point a vet towards the right test.

This is where having a laboratory inside the clinic makes a practical difference. When samples are sent away, results come back days later; run in-house, a blood count and biochemistry panel are ready within the same visit, in roughly half an hour. For a feverish dog that has had a tick, that is the difference between starting treatment today and waiting until tomorrow. In severe cases hours matter, which is why our 24/7 emergency service assesses these patients without delay.

How Often, at What Age?

  • Puppies and kittens. A baseline check at the start of the vaccination programme, plus parasite and infectious disease screening if the animal came from the street. Planned alongside the kitten vaccination schedule.
  • Healthy adults (1–7 years). Once a year, at the same visit as the general check-up and boosters. The aim is not to hunt for disease but to record a personal normal range.
  • Over seven years. Every six months. Kidney, thyroid and liver values change faster from this age, and a six-month interval catches the drift early.
  • Animals with chronic conditions. At the interval your vet sets. Where medication is ongoing, these tests also monitor its load on the organs.
  • Before every operation. Regardless of age.

Practical Details Before Your Appointment

Most biochemistry panels ask for 8 to 12 hours without food, because the fat that appears in blood after a meal can distort several values. Do not withhold water. Puppies, kittens and diabetic animals follow different fasting rules, so say so when you book.

A broad panel usually needs 1 to 3 millilitres of blood — for a medium-sized cat, less than one per cent of total blood volume. The procedure normally takes under a minute and needs no sedation. If your pet is anxious at the clinic, put a familiar blanket from home in the carrier; a known smell noticeably reduces stress during handling.

If your pet has been tested at another clinic before, bring those results with you. An old value is the single most useful piece of information for interpreting a new one.

What a “Borderline” Value Means

A single value falling just outside the reference range rarely makes a diagnosis by itself. Stress, fasting time, a recent meal or the way the sample was handled can all move the numbers. Your vet reads the result together with the physical examination, the animal's age and breed, and any earlier tests.

The usual next step is to repeat that one value a few weeks later. The direction between two measurements — rising, stable, or back to where it was — tells you far more than a single reading. Asking your vet rather than interpreting the printout at home protects you from unnecessary worry and from false reassurance alike.

In Conclusion

A blood test is not a last resort once your pet is ill; it is a reference point held in reserve before that happens. An examination shows you today, while testing shows you the direction of travel. A simple panel once a year builds, over time, a picture no single examination could ever produce, and in most conditions it opens the door to early intervention. When surgery is involved, screening is the single step that reduces risk the most.

Frequently Asked Questions

My cat or dog looks healthy. Is a blood test still worth doing?

Yes, and this is where it is most valuable. Cats and dogs instinctively hide illness; roughly two thirds of kidney tissue can be lost before any outward sign appears. A test taken while your pet is well records that individual animal's personal normal range. Compared against a result a year later, a meaningful shift is caught early even when both values sit inside the published reference range.

Is pre-anaesthetic blood testing really necessary?

Anaesthetic drugs are broken down by the liver and cleared by the kidneys. Dosing without knowing the capacity of those two organs carries a risk of prolonged recovery or organ damage, even in an animal that looks perfectly healthy. The panel also reveals clotting problems and hidden infection. If results are normal, surgery proceeds as planned; if not, the dose is adjusted, fluid support is given, or the procedure is postponed. This applies to routine operations such as neutering too.

Does my pet need to fast before the test?

Most biochemistry panels ask for 8 to 12 hours without food, because fat in the blood after a meal can distort several values. Do not withhold water. In an emergency we do not wait for fasting; the test is run and the result interpreted with that in mind. Puppies, kittens and diabetic animals follow different rules, so mention this when booking.

How much blood is taken, and will it harm my pet?

A broad panel usually needs 1 to 3 millilitres. For a medium-sized cat that is less than one per cent of total blood volume and has no noticeable effect on a healthy animal. The procedure normally takes under a minute and does not require sedation. Blood is usually drawn from a front leg or the jugular vein.

How long do results take?

Where a clinic runs its own laboratory, a blood count and biochemistry panel are generally ready within the same visit, in about half an hour, and rapid kits for tick-borne disease and Leishmaniasis give an answer in minutes. When samples must be sent away, results take days. In an urgent case that difference decides whether treatment starts the same day or waits until tomorrow.

One value came back just outside the reference range. Should I worry?

A single borderline value rarely makes a diagnosis on its own. Stress, fasting time, a recent meal or the way the sample was handled can all move numbers. Your vet reads the result alongside the physical examination, the animal's age and any earlier tests. The usual next step is simply to repeat that one value a few weeks later, because the direction of travel tells you far more than a single measurement.
