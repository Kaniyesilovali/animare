Limping in Dogs and Cats: Is an X-Ray Needed?

29 August 2026 · Dr. Gökay Yeşilovalı, Veterinarian

You get up in the morning and your dog will not put a paw down, or your cat has stopped jumping onto the sofa. The first question is always the same: will it settle on its own, or should you go to the clinic now? This article sorts the causes of limping by age, sets out plainly which signs cannot wait, and explains where an X-ray fits into the decision — because not every limp needs imaging, but when it does, nothing else takes its place.

In short

  • No weight on the leg, a limb at an abnormal angle, obvious swelling or an accompanying fever cannot wait.
  • With a mild, improving limp, resting and observing for 24 hours is reasonable; no improvement in 48 hours means an examination.
  • In cats every limp is taken more seriously — cats hide pain far better than dogs.
  • X-rays show bone: fractures, dislocations, arthritis, suspected tumours. Ligament and tendon tears do not appear.
  • Most images need no sedation; a few seconds of stillness is enough.
  • Never give human painkillers — paracetamol is fatal to cats.

Which Signs Cannot Wait

A good proportion of limps are simple strains that settle with rest. Some findings, though, do not tolerate lost hours. If any of the following is present, your pet needs to be seen the same day:

  • No weight on the leg at all. An animal walking on three legs is not in mild pain.
  • An abnormally held limb. An unnatural angle, a dangling leg or a rotated foot suggests a fracture or dislocation.
  • Obvious swelling or heat, particularly over a single joint.
  • Crying out when touched. A gentle animal suddenly trying to bite means the same thing.
  • Fever, lethargy or loss of appetite alongside the limp. This is no longer just a leg problem.
  • A history of a road accident or a fall from height. Internal injury is possible even when the outside looks fine.

Do not delay with these. Call our clinic before you set off, so the team can prepare while you travel.

Where the limp is mild, steadily improving and your pet is otherwise bright, restricting movement and watching for 24 hours is reasonable. No stairs, no jumping, no running; dogs go out on a short lead for toilet breaks only. If there is no improvement within 48 hours, or things get worse in the meantime, book an examination.

Common Causes by Age

Puppies and Young Animals

In fast-growing large breeds, developmental problems dominate: hip and elbow dysplasia, growth plate injuries, and the bone pain particular to the growth period. In this group a limp may move from leg to leg, settle with rest and then return. Early diagnosis matters, because weight control and adjusted exercise slow the progression considerably.

Adults

Soft tissue injuries are what we see most: muscle strains, sprains, cuts to the paw pad. In dogs, a sudden hind-leg limp that does not resolve is often a cruciate ligament rupture; the dog is typically running when it suddenly starts to hop and then touches the ground only with its toes. Grass seeds, thorns and other foreign bodies lodged between the toes are also frequent at this age and can turn into an abscess.

Older Animals

After the age of seven, osteoarthritis is the first thing to consider. The signs creep in: stiffness in the morning, avoiding stairs, reluctance to play, and in cats giving up jumping to high places. Owners usually read this as “slowing down with age” — but it is treatable pain. Persistent pain in a single limb that worsens at night and comes with weight loss points to something more serious, such as a bone tumour, and imaging should not be delayed.

When an X-Ray Is Needed

An X-ray is not the first step. The examination comes first: the leg is assessed joint by joint by hand to establish exactly where the pain sits and whether there is swelling or abnormal movement. In many cases the diagnosis is made there and imaging is unnecessary. The table below summarises what the decision rests on.

Situation Imaging decision
History of trauma (vehicle, fall, impact)X-ray taken; chest and abdomen imaged too if indicated.
No weight-bearing at allX-ray taken — fracture and dislocation must be separated.
Abnormality felt on examinationX-ray taken.
Limp lasting more than two weeksX-ray taken; a chronic joint problem is looked for.
Older animal, persistent pain in one limbX-ray not delayed.
Mild, improving limp with no traumaRest and observe first; image if it does not settle.
Shifting limp with feverBlood test first; tick-borne joint inflammation considered.

What an X-Ray Shows — and What It Misses

X-rays show bone extremely well. Fractures and hairline cracks, dislocations, narrowed joint spaces, the bony spurs of arthritis and suspected bone tumours are all revealed this way. It is also the first tool for a swallowed foreign body and for assessing the chest.

What it misses matters just as much. Ligament, tendon and muscle tears do not appear directly on an X-ray. With a cruciate rupture, the diagnosis rests largely on the physical examination; the X-ray is taken to show joint fluid and any accompanying bony change. For soft tissue and internal organs, ultrasound is the more suitable tool. Our radiology service has digital X-ray and ultrasound together, so we can direct you to whichever the examination calls for.

Being digital has a practical consequence: the image appears on screen within seconds, contrast and magnification can be adjusted afterwards, and a region can be re-examined without re-exposing the animal. That reduces both the number of images taken and the time your pet spends at the clinic.

What Happens During Imaging

It is a short procedure. Your pet is positioned on the table and held still for a few seconds so the image is sharp, which gentle handling usually achieves. At least two different views are normally taken, because a hairline crack can hide when viewed from a single angle. Where comparison helps, the sound leg is imaged as well.

Sedation is not needed for most images. We recommend light sedation when the pain is severe, when correct positioning would mean extending a painful limb, or for views such as hip assessment that need full muscle relaxation. If that is the plan, we explain why beforehand. Where sedation is arranged, your pet is asked to come in fasted and a blood test is usually run to check organ capacity first.

Results are discussed in the same visit. If a finding requires surgery, we set out the options and the expected recovery time that day and, where needed, plan the procedure with you.

Why Limping Is Missed in Cats

Cats conceal pain far better than dogs. Instead of an obvious limp they show changes in behaviour: giving up jumping to high places, struggling to get in and out of the litter tray, grooming less or, conversely, over-licking one area, hiding, and becoming irritable. These signs are easily written off as old age.

In reality, joint disease in older cats is much more common than most owners assume. If the places your cat can reach are shrinking, that is rarely a change of character — it is usually pain, and it can be treated. Simple changes at home help too: a litter tray with a lower side, a step up to the bed, and food and water placed where no jumping is required.

What We See Most in Cyprus

Some causes are markedly more common on the island. In summer, hot asphalt and sand burn and blister the paw pads; the limp an owner assumes came from nowhere often traces back to an afternoon walk. Our article on summer heat and pet safety explains how to test the ground temperature with your hand.

Dogs walked through dry grassland pick up grass seeds and thorns between the toes; what starts as a mild limp worsens as an abscess develops. Another important cause is tick-borne Ehrlichiosis, which can involve the joints and present as a limp that shifts from leg to leg. In that picture a blood test is more informative than an X-ray — which is why examination, imaging and laboratory work are assessed together.

What to Do at Home Until the Appointment

Do not give painkillers. Human medicines cause serious poisoning in cats and dogs: paracetamol is fatal to cats even in small doses, and ibuprofen and aspirin can cause stomach bleeding and kidney damage in both species. Our article on toxic foods and substances has the detail. Masking pain carries a second risk: the animal carries on using the injured leg and the damage grows.

What to do is simple. Restrict movement — take dogs out on a short lead for toilet breaks only, and keep cats in a single room. Prevent stairs, jumping onto furniture and running. If there is swelling, an ice pack wrapped in a thin cloth can be applied for no more than fifteen minutes; never place it directly on the skin. If you can see a cut or an embedded object in the paw pad, do not try to remove it yourself — stop your pet licking it and bring them in.

Make a note, too: when did the limp start, which leg, does it ease with rest, is it worse on stairs? If you can, take a short video of your pet walking. Animals are often tense at the clinic and may not show the limp at all; ten seconds of footage from home makes the examination considerably easier.

In Conclusion

A limp is not a diagnosis but a signal; behind it may be a simple strain, or a fracture, or joint disease. What tells them apart is the examination — the X-ray is the tool that completes that decision, not the starting point. With an animal that cannot bear weight, holds a limb abnormally or has a fever, do not wait. With a mild limp, restrict movement, observe for twenty-four hours, give no medication, and book an examination if it has not settled.

Frequently Asked Questions

My dog has a slight limp. Can I wait a few days?

With a mild limp that is steadily improving, resting and observing for 24 hours is reasonable. These signs cannot wait, however: refusing to put any weight on the leg, a limb held at an abnormal angle, obvious swelling, crying out when touched, fever or lethargy alongside the limp, or a limp that has not improved at all in 48 hours. In cats every limp is treated more seriously, because cats hide pain far better than dogs.

Is an X-ray taken for every limp?

No. The examination comes first: the leg is assessed joint by joint by hand to find exactly where the pain is and whether there is swelling or abnormal movement. For something like a soft tissue strain, imaging may not be needed at all. An X-ray is requested when there is a history of trauma, the animal cannot bear weight, something abnormal is felt on examination, the limp has lasted more than two weeks, or an older animal has persistent pain in one limb.

What does an X-ray show, and what does it miss?

X-rays show bone very well: fractures, cracks, dislocations, narrowed joint spaces, arthritic changes and suspected bone tumours. Ligament, tendon and muscle tears, by contrast, are not directly visible and can only be inferred indirectly. With a cruciate ligament rupture the diagnosis rests largely on the physical examination, and the X-ray is taken to show accompanying changes in the bone. For soft tissue and internal organs, ultrasound is the better tool.

Will my pet be sedated for an X-ray?

Usually not. The animal needs to stay still for a few seconds so the image is sharp, and gentle handling is normally enough. Light sedation is recommended when the pain is severe, when the correct position would mean extending a painful limb, or for views such as hip assessment that require full muscle relaxation. If sedation is needed we explain why beforehand.

Can I give my limping pet a painkiller at home?

No. Human painkillers cause serious poisoning in cats and dogs. Paracetamol is fatal to cats even in small doses, and ibuprofen and aspirin can cause stomach bleeding and kidney damage in both species. Masking the pain also has a second drawback: the animal keeps using the injured leg and the damage worsens. Restrict movement until the examination and give no medication.

Are there causes of limping specific to Cyprus?

Several are noticeably more common here. In summer, hot asphalt and sand cause burns and blisters on the paw pads. Dogs walked through dry grassland pick up grass seeds and thorns between the toes, which can develop into an abscess. Tick-borne Ehrlichiosis can involve the joints and appear as a limp that moves from leg to leg; in that picture a blood test is more informative than an X-ray. This is why examination, imaging and laboratory work are assessed together.
